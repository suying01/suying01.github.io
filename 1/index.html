Project 1

Project Overview

Each digitized negative is a single tall grayscale image: the same scene shot through blue, green, and red filters and stacked top to bottom. Splitting it into thirds gives three grayscale channels that are almost, but not quite, in register. The camera moved slightly, or the plates shifted during scanning, so each channel needs to be nudged back into place before it means anything as color. Hence,we find the (x, y) shift that brings G and R into register with B, then stack the three as color channels. No two images need the same shift, so it has to be found automatically for every plate.

Single-scale search on the low-res JPEGs

The simplest approach is to try every whole pixel shift in a window. [−15, 15] in both x and y which is 961 candidates and keep whichever one makes the channel look most like the blue reference. Two scoring functions do this well:

Normalized cross-correlation (used throughout this project)
\[ \mathrm{NCC}(A, B)=\frac{(A-\mathrm{mean}(A))}{\|A-\mathrm{mean}(A)\|}\cdot\frac{(B-\mathrm{mean}(B))}{\|B-\mathrm{mean}(B)\|} \]

We take the shift that maximizes this.

Euclidean / L2 distance (equivalent alternative)
\[ \mathrm{L_2}(A, B)=\sqrt{\sum (A-B)^2} \]

We take the shift that minimizes this.

Before scoring, each candidate crop is trimmed by about 10% on every side. The scanned plates have dark, uneven borders that have nothing to do with scene content, and without cropping them out the search aligns the black edges instead of the picture.

Multi-scale pyramid alignment on the full example set

The full-resolution .tif scans are roughly 90× the pixel count of the JPEGs, and their true displacements are often larger than the ±15 window can find directly at full resolution. A coarse-to-fine image pyramid solves both problems: downsample repeatedly until the image is a few hundred pixels across, exhaustively search there with the same NCC/L2 metric, then walk back up the pyramid, doubling the estimate and refining it with a small local search at each level. The same pipeline, unmodified, was run on all fourteen example plates (the three JPEGs are included here too, for completeness — their pyramid results are identical to the single-scale results above, since an image this small only produces one pyramid level):

Bells & whistles: gradient-based matching for emir.tif

Raw-pixel NCC assumes matching content has matching brightness across channels. That assumption breaks on saturated color: the emir's blue-and-gold robe is bright in blue, dim in red, so the red channel's "brightest match" for the robe isn't near the robe at all. Running the pipeline on emir.tif with raw-pixel NCC (the same metric used for every other plate) produced a badly misaligned result — visible ghosting, two overlapping figures. Switching only this image's scoring function from raw pixel intensity to Sobel gradient magnitude (sqrt(Sobel_x² + Sobel_y²)) fixed it. Edges like the outline of a sleeve, the border of a tile, the frame of a door, tend to show up in all three color channels even when brightness doesn't, because an edge is a change in reflectance, not an absolute color. No other part of the search or pyramid code changes; only what gets compared changes.

This was necessary only for emir.tif. Every other plate, including the other colorful ones (icon, siren, self_portrait), aligned cleanly under plain raw-pixel NCC.

Failure cases

All eighteen plates above (fourteen provided examples plus four of my own) aligned cleanly with no visible ghosting or doubled edges, once the gradient-based metric was applied to emir.tif. The one situation this approach cannot fix is real motion between the three exposures — a person, animal, or water moving while Prokudin-Gorskii shot the red, green, and blue plates seconds apart. In that case the misalignment isn't a shift at all, it's a change in the scene itself, so no single (x, y) translation can correct it. None of the images tested here showed enough motion for this to be a problem.
